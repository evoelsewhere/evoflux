# Content-derived presentation grammar

Use this reference before choosing layouts or building native slides. The deck's
form must grow from its communication job, evidence, brand, supplied images,
and presentation setting. A named aesthetic can narrow the search, but it is
not a recipe.

## Derive the system

Answer these questions first:

1. **Communication job:** who is the audience, what should they understand,
   believe, choose, approve, discuss, or do, and what central takeaway earns
   that outcome?
2. **Narrative role:** is this slide a promise, transition, assertion,
   evidence, comparison, mechanism, quote, synthesis, or decision?
3. **Viewing distance:** will it be read closely, presented on a laptop, or
   projected across a room?
4. **Emotional temperature:** should it feel bold, calm, authoritative,
   humane, urgent, playful, or restrained?
5. **Capacity:** what can the audience understand in the available time, and
   what belongs on another slide or in notes?
6. **Visual motif:** which object, structure, image, or metaphor is distinctive
   to this content rather than reusable decoration?

Then declare one compact system:

- canvas and safe area;
- primary, muted, structural, and semantic colors;
- display, body, numeric, and source-note typography;
- spacing rhythm, grid, image treatment, and recurring anchors;
- 4–5 layout families, each tied to a communication job;
- density and contrast limits for the actual audience and language.

## Derive color and typography

Use **sample → converge → justify**:

1. sample color from brand assets, meaningful images, or cultural context;
2. converge on 2–3 chromatic colors plus a neutral scale;
3. state why each color belongs to this subject.

Choose typography in this order: content type → language mix → emotional
temperature → font family. Use a strong display/body contrast only when it
supports the story. Test Vietnamese and CJK glyphs when present. Use tabular
figures for data. Shorten copy or change layout before shrinking text.

## Build slide rhythm

- Give every slide one communication job and a takeaway title.
- Give every slide one dominant visual or typographic protagonist.
- Reuse title, grid, footer, and source-note anchors across the deck.
- Vary adjacent silhouettes, density, image/text balance, and occasional
  background changes without changing the system.
- Prefer one clear composition over collections of UI panels. Avoid card grids,
  pills, tabs, badges, buttons, navigation chrome, and dashboard styling unless
  the content is genuinely modular and the interaction metaphor is necessary.
- Alternate broad orientation, evidence, and breathing-space moments; avoid
  more than two dense evidence slides in sequence.
- Keep the title slide minimal. Open with context, purpose, stakes, or a useful
  question; close by resolving that opening with a decision, action, synthesis,
  implication, or productive question—not a generic thank-you slide.

For decks of five or more slides, author two structurally different showcase
slides before batching the rest: usually the opening plus the most demanding
content, data, or image slide. Confirm hierarchy, type, color, spacing, image
treatment, and density from their rendered previews.

## Content integrity

- Use real claims, metrics, quotations, logos, product images, and UI captures.
- Never invent content to fill space. Split, simplify, or leave an explicit
  placeholder in a draft.
- Prefer assertion-and-evidence composition to decorative bullets.
- Turn evidence into meaning: state why each important fact, metric, chart, or
  example matters to this audience and how it changes the conclusion.
- Use SVG for icons, geometric structure, and data visualization—not as a
  substitute for a real person, product, place, or scene.
- Keep audience-facing slides free of production notes and design rationale.
- Use direct, natural copy. Remove timing scaffolds, talk tracks, internal
  instructions, repeated slogans, and content that does not advance the story.
