# Writing & Deliverables

## Overview

The metric for a deliverable is what it does for its reader, not the effort behind it. A report nobody can scan, a summary that buries the answer, an email that takes three reads to parse — these fail regardless of how much work went in. This skill turns raw material or a brief into a finished artifact that lands its point fast, supports its claims, and is immediately usable.

Default to **tight**. Most drafts are too long, too hedged, and too slow to reach the point. The work is as much cutting as composing.

## When to Use

- Writing or drafting a document, report, brief, memo, email, or post
- Summarising or distilling source material into something usable
- Rewriting/editing existing text for clarity, length, or audience
- Assembling a structured deliverable (spec, outline, table, dataset, config) into a clean final form

**When NOT to use:**

- The task is to *decide* or *analyse* (use decision-analysis) or to *research* facts (use research-and-fact-checking) — those produce the substance; this skill packages it
- A one-line reply where structure adds nothing

## The Process

Copy this checklist when applying the skill:

```
Deliverable cycle:
- [ ] Step 1: SPEC — fixed audience, purpose, format, length
- [ ] Step 2: OUTLINE — conclusion first, then ordered by reader need
- [ ] Step 3: DRAFT — one idea per paragraph, concrete words, tables where they help
- [ ] Step 4: EDIT — read as the recipient, cut everything that doesn't serve the purpose
- [ ] Step 5: FINISH — complete, valid, correctly named and located
```

### Step 1: SPEC — Pin down the deliverable

Before writing a word, fix four things. Guessing here is the most common cause of a deliverable that misses:

- **Audience** — who reads it, and what do they already know? (Determines how much to explain and which jargon is safe.)
- **Purpose** — what should it make them understand or do? Everything that doesn't serve this gets cut.
- **Format** — memo, table, slide outline, email, structured file? Pick the shape that carries the content best.
- **Length** — tight by default. State a target if one is implied.

If any of these is genuinely ambiguous and the choice changes the output materially, state your interpretation in one line and proceed.

### Step 2: OUTLINE — Conclusion first

Lead with the answer, recommendation, or main point. Readers decide in the first lines whether to keep going; making them hunt for the payoff is the cardinal sin. Order the rest by **what the audience needs**, not by the order you discovered it. A quick outline (even three bullets) prevents the meandering draft that has to be rewritten anyway.

```
Bad order:  background → process → caveats → ... → (finally) the recommendation
Good order: recommendation → the 2 reasons that matter → essential caveats → detail
```

### Step 3: DRAFT — Write for the reader

- **One idea per paragraph.** If a paragraph turns, split it.
- **Concrete nouns and verbs** over abstractions and nominalisations. "The migration deletes 1,200 rows" beats "data reduction will occur."
- **Cut hedging and throat-clearing.** "It is important to note that" / "in order to" / "basically" add nothing.
- **Reach for structure** — a table for anything comparative, a numbered list for anything sequential, headings so the piece is scannable.
- **Match the register.** A status ping, a formal report, and a quick reply call for different tones; fit the context.

### Step 4: EDIT — Cut as the recipient

Read the draft back as the person receiving it, with their time and priorities. Then:

- Delete anything that doesn't serve the purpose — including good sentences that wandered in.
- Tighten every remaining sentence; remove words whose absence doesn't change the meaning.
- Verify each claim is supported or sourced; replace assertions with evidence where it matters.
- Check the piece is **scannable**: can a busy reader get the gist from headings and first lines alone?

Editing is where mediocre drafts become good deliverables. Budget real time for it.

### Step 5: FINISH — Ship it clean

Deliver complete, valid output saved to the right place with a clear name. Match the conventions of surrounding files — formatting, heading style, naming. Never leave an artifact half-written or in a broken state; if you wrote a file, re-read it to confirm it's correct and complete.

## Principles

- **Bottom line up front.** State the answer, then support it.
- **Scannable beats dense.** Headings, short paragraphs, and lists let a busy reader extract value in seconds.
- **Precision beats length.** Every removed word that doesn't change the meaning makes the rest stronger.
- **No unsupported claims.** If a statement carries weight, it carries a reason or a source.
- **Format follows content.** Comparative → table. Sequential → numbered list. Narrative → prose. Don't force one shape on everything.

## Quality Checklist

Before declaring done:

- [ ] The first lines tell the reader the main point.
- [ ] Audience, purpose, format, and length are all served.
- [ ] Structure is scannable (headings / lists where they help).
- [ ] Every claim is supported; no filler, hedging, or repetition.
- [ ] Tone fits the audience and context.
- [ ] The artifact is complete, correctly formatted, and named/located sensibly.

## Common Rationalizations

| Rationalization | Reality |
|---|---|
| "More detail makes it more thorough" | More detail makes it *longer*. Thorough is the reader getting the point and the support, fast. |
| "I'll put the conclusion at the end for impact" | The reader leaves before the end. Lead with the conclusion; use the body to support it. |
| "The first draft is good enough" | First drafts are for the writer. The edit pass is what makes it good for the reader. |
| "They'll figure out the structure" | They won't — they'll skim and miss the point. Structure is your job, not theirs. |
